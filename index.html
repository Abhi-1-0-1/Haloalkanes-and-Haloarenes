Alkyl Halide Types

Halogen is on an sp³ carbon.

TypeStructureExample
Primary (1°) \(R\text{-}CH_2\text{-}X\) \(CH_3CH_2Cl\)
Secondary (2°) \(R_2\text{-}CH\text{-}X\) \((CH_3)_2CHBr\)
Tertiary (3°) \(R_3\text{-}C\text{-}X\) \((CH_3)_3CCl\)
Allylic \(C=C\text{-}CH_2\text{-}X\) \(CH_2=CH\text{-}CH_2Cl\)
Benzylic \(Ar\text{-}CH_2\text{-}X\) \(C_6H_5CH_2Br\)
Vinylic \(C=C\text{-}X\) (on sp²) \(CH_2=CHCl\)
Aryl Halide \(Ar\text{-}X\) (ring sp²) \(C_6H_5Br\)
📌
Key Distinctions to Remember
Allylic vs Vinylic Allylic: X is on the sp³ carbon next to the double bond. Vinylic: X is directly on the sp² carbon of the double bond. Crucial difference for reactivity!
Benzylic vs Aryl Benzylic: X is on the sp³ carbon attached to the ring (–CH₂X). Aryl: X is directly on the ring's sp² carbon. Aryl halides are much less reactive in SN reactions.
Chapter 2 Nomenclature — IUPAC & Common
📖
IUPAC System Rules
  • Name = halogen prefix (fluoro/chloro/bromo/iodo) + parent alkane
  • Rule 1: Number the chain to give the halogen the lowest possible locant
  • Rule 2: If multiple halogens, list in alphabetical order (e.g., 1-bromo-2-chloro…)
  • When numbering conflict: lower set of locants wins at first point of difference
🏷️

Boiling Point Trend

Higher molar mass → stronger van der Waals forces → higher BP

Branching Effect More branching → less surface area → weaker vdW → lower BP. n-Butyl chloride boils higher than tert-Butyl chloride.
💧
Solubility & Density
  • Water: RX ≈ insoluble — cannot form H-bonds with water (no O–H or N–H)
  • Organic solvents: RX dissolves readily (like dissolves like)
  • Density order: \(RI > RBr > RCl\) — denser halogens give denser compounds; also increases with number of halogens
  • \(RF\) is often lighter than water (despite F being dense) due to C–F bond character

Chapter 5 Nucleophilic Substitution
🔀
SN1 Mechanism Steps
1

Ionisation — Rate Determining Step

The C–X bond breaks heterolytically. The leaving group departs with both electrons, forming a carbocation intermediate. Rate depends only on [RX]. Favoured by polar protic solvents that stabilise ions.

2

Nucleophilic Attack

The nucleophile attacks the planar sp² carbocation from both faces equally → racemisation (50:50 enantiomers). This is the fast step.

!

Consequence: Rearrangement Possible

Because a carbocation forms, 1,2-hydride or alkyl shifts can occur if they yield a more stable carbocation. SN2 never rearranges.

⚡
SN2 Mechanism — Concerted
1

Back-Side Attack

Strong nucleophile attacks the carbon 180° opposite the leaving group simultaneously as the leaving group departs. One single step — no intermediate.

2

Walden Inversion (Umbrella Flip)

The three remaining substituents invert like an umbrella. Complete inversion of configuration — R becomes S and vice versa. 100% stereospecific.

⚖️
Nucleophilicity vs. Basicity Rules
Rule 1 — Kinetic vs Thermodynamic Nucleophilicity is a rate (kinetic) property measured by how fast it attacks. Basicity is an equilibrium (thermodynamic) property. They don't always correlate!
Rule 2 — Steric Bulk Bulky base (e.g., \(t\text{-}BuO^-\)) → high basicity but LOW nucleophilicity. Steric hindrance blocks the backside attack. It acts as a base, not a nucleophile → favours E2.
Rule 3 — Group 17 Trend (Protic Solvent) Nucleophilicity: \(I^- > Br^- > Cl^- > F^-\) (larger, more polarisable, less solvated). Basicity reverses: \(F^- > Cl^- > Br^- > I^-\). In aprotic solvents, order becomes basicity order.
Rule 4 — Charge Effect Anionic nucleophiles are always better than their neutral counterparts: \(OH^- > H_2O\), \(RO^- > ROH\), \(NH_2^- > NH_3\).
Chapter 6 Elimination Reactions (E2)
📐
E2 Technical Specs
ParameterDetail
Kinetics2nd order: Rate ∝ [RX][Base] — both substrate and base in rate law
MechanismConcerted, one-step: H and X leave simultaneously (anti-periplanar)
StereochemistryAnti-elimination: H and X must be anti-periplanar (180°)
IntermediateNone — pentacoordinate transition state only
RearrangementImpossible (no carbocation formed)
Leaving group order\(RI > RBr > RCl > RF\) — bond breaking is rate-determining
🎯
Regioselectivity — Which Alkene Forms?
Zaitsev (Saytzeff) Rule — More Substituted Product Small, unhindered base (e.g., alc. KOH, \(EtO^-\)) → removes H from the carbon with fewer H's → gives the more substituted (more stable) alkene. Most thermodynamically stable product.
Hofmann Rule — Less Substituted Product Bulky base (e.g., \(t\text{-}BuOK\)) → cannot approach the hindered carbon → removes H from the least hindered carbon → gives less substituted alkene (Hofmann product).
Rate ∝ Alkene Stability More stable product alkene → lower transition state energy → faster rate. Tetrasubstituted > trisubstituted > disubstituted > monosubstituted > ethylene.
🆚
When Does Elimination Beat Substitution?
  • Bulky base/nucleophile → E2 over SN2
  • High temperature → E2 favoured (entropy-driven, two products from one)
  • Tertiary substrate + strong base → E2 (SN2 blocked by steric hindrance; SN1 competes)
  • Strong, non-bulky nucleophile + primary substrate → SN2 dominates
  • \(NaNH_2\): extremely strong base → forces E2 even on difficult substrates

Chapter 8 Aryl Halide Inertness in SN
🛡️
4 Reasons Aryl Halides Don't Undergo SN
1

Resonance Donation

The lone pairs on X delocalise into the ring π system → partial C–X double bond character → shorter, stronger bond. Cannot be broken easily.

2

sp² Carbon is More Electronegative

The aryl carbon is sp² hybridised → higher s-character → more electronegative → holds onto the bonding electrons more tightly → shorter, stronger C–X bond vs. sp³.

3

Phenyl Cation Instability (blocks SN1)

For SN1, a phenyl cation would have to form at an sp² carbon. This is extremely unstable (sp² orbital can't overlap with empty orbital efficiently) → SN1 is impossible for aryl halides.

4

π Cloud Repulsion (blocks SN2)

The electron-rich aromatic π cloud above and below the ring electrostatically repels incoming nucleophiles → backside attack required for SN2 is completely blocked.
